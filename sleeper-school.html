Sleeper School

A story from Don't Let Them
Rule #1: Don't get touched.
Shiremanstown, PA — 6th Period Science

You were counting down the minutes. Something unseen was counting down with you.

The room smelled like formaldehyde had replaced the air itself. Outside, your hometown sat calm and ordinary under the late-afternoon sun. Everything was normal. Boring. Until it wasn't.

Principal Wilson stumbled through the door mid-lesson. Professionally dressed, staggering like he'd had a very bad afternoon. A weird symbol marked his forehead. His eyes were open but vacant—awareness drained clean out of them.

Mrs. Phillips stepped toward him. "Mr. Wilson?" His hand bumped hers. She went down behind her desk with a dull thud.

The room froze. Then the chain reaction started. One by one, the moment anyone touched Wilson—or each other—they folded. Like marionettes with their strings cut. The classroom floor was filling up with warm bodies.

You told yourself they were still warm. Like that helped.

You pushed back from your table. The door was blocked—a pile of collapsed students between you and the hallway. Across the room, Taylor's green eyes found yours. She looked like she wanted to ask you something, then remembered just in time that she hated you.

A ceiling tile crashed down between you, exploding into white dust.

The ones on the floor were starting to stir.

How do you get out?
A Natural Reaction

Their hand was right there. You grabbed it.

The cold hit first—not like cold air, like cold water, like something reaching up through your fingers and pulling down. The classroom tilted. Your knees went soft.

Oh, you thought. So that's how it works.

The floor came up fast. It wasn't painful exactly—more like the moment before sleep when your body decides it's done negotiating. You heard Taylor shout your name from somewhere very far away.

Then nothing.

Just warmth, and quiet, and the faint smell of formaldehyde.

And somewhere in the dark behind your eyes, something that wasn't you started to wake up.

The Window

The window handle wouldn't budge. You strained harder. Behind you, the stirring bodies were finding their feet, arms outstretching, eyes glassy and wrong.

The latch snapped open. Cold air punched through like it had been waiting all day. Blinds cracked against the glass in rhythm with your heartbeat.

The frame stuck halfway. You shoved. Wood groaned, paint flakes rained on your sleeve. Outside, the wind screamed through the gap—damp concrete and winter grass.

Taylor scrambled across the lab tables toward you, boots clattering on black tabletops, narrowly dodging a hand that swiped at her.

"It looks a little far," she said, peering down at the roof below.

Really, Taylor? Now's the time for math homework?

"You'll survive," you said, and hoisted yourself up.

Your landing sent a dull thunk through the air. You wobbled, pushed your glasses up, and gave an exaggerated bow up at Taylor. You couldn't help yourself.

Her pale face hovered in the window. Brown ponytail whipping in the wind. Behind her, fingers brushed the air inches from her backpack.

She's about to jump. Do you help?
A Gentlemanly Mistake

You reached up to steady her landing. She came down hard, one foot catching your shoulder, and the impact sent you both tumbling sideways onto the rooftop gravel.

You lay there for a second, winded, staring at the sky. Taylor was already on her feet, brushing grit from her palms, looking at you with an expression that was equal parts annoyed and mortified.

"I didn't need help," she said.

"I know," you said, from the ground.

Somewhere below, a door banged open. The shuffling that followed was not the sound of someone who needed help. You'd cost yourselves fifteen seconds—and on a day like today, fifteen seconds was everything.

By the time you got to your feet, the route down to the parking lot was gone.

She had always been going to land on her own. You knew that. You'd always known that.

Fifteen seconds.

The Door

You mapped it in your head: step left of Martinez, right of the two freshmen, don't clip the backpack on the floor. Six feet of human obstacle course and then the hallway.

You made it four feet.

The problem wasn't the ones on the floor. It was the ones getting back up. Wilson's hand closed around your ankle from behind a toppled stool—ice cold, impossibly strong. The cold crawled up your leg like current, pulling your strength down with it.

Your eyelids hit a hundred pounds each.

Not like this, you thought. Not on the science room floor.

Then someone yanked your hood hard enough to choke you, and the grip broke. Taylor. Standing over you, looking furious—which was, somehow, reassuring. She pointed at the window without a word.

You didn't argue.

You're back at the window. What now?
Thirty Seconds

Thirty seconds. That was all.

Your locker combination took two tries because your hands were shaking. Twelve seconds. The coat was jammed behind your gym bag. Eight more. You yanked it free.

When you turned around, the hallway was full.

Not a few stragglers. Full. A slow tide of blank-faced students and faculty moving in your direction with the collective purpose of something that doesn't need to hurry because it already knows where you're going.

You looked at your coat.

You looked at the hallway.

It was a nice coat.

You dropped it and ran. You didn't make it far.

The coat was still there the next morning, folded neatly over the handle of your locker.

You were not.

The coat wasn't worth it.

The Parking Lot

The parking lot was chaos. Empty cars at odd angles, doors yawning open. People staggered between the vehicles—arms out, faces blank. You knew them. Mrs. Schaefer from the library, still clutching her keys. Danny Wu from soccer, jersey torn.

Then you spotted the bus. A driver—short, curly-haired, half a cigarette in her mouth—was waving survivors toward it. There weren't many. The bus sat nose-pointed at the street with an open path to anywhere else.

"Get on, we gotta go!" she shouted.

You climbed the steps. Sank into a green bench seat at the back. The bus smelled of vinyl and nicotine. Outside the window, the disorder felt like it was happening to someone else. You were almost safe. Removed.

Taylor sat a few rows ahead. She seemed relieved not to be stuck with you. The feeling was mutual.

The driver—Becky—had gathered six students. She was scanning for more when a shadow passed over the roof. Wide and impossible. The bus groaned.

The smallest kid on the bus stood up. A middle schooler. His eyes looked like smoke was forming inside them. A symbol appeared on his forehead—the same one Wilson had. His hand reached for the girl beside him. She went limp.

Becky unbuckled and rushed forward. The moment she touched him, she went down too.

Here we go again.

What do you do?
Alone

You moved fast across the rooftop, dropped to the sidewalk, and cut toward the street before any of the shuffling shapes in the parking lot could clock you.

It felt smart. For about two blocks.

Then the adrenaline thinned out, and you became aware of how quiet it was. Your sneakers on the pavement. Your breath. Nothing else. Every house shut. Every storefront dark.

You saw an elderly couple shuffle off a porch ahead—same blank eyes, same outstretched arms, same mark on one forehead. You dodged them. Cut toward Main Street. Okay. Fine. You could handle three.

Then there were more. Coming from a side street. From a driveway. From a bus stop. Not fast—never fast—but there was no gap in them anymore, just a slow wall of people who used to have names you knew.

You backed against a brick wall. Your phone had no signal. The bus you'd ignored was somewhere behind you, already gone.

"You do you," you'd said.

Great plan, Ford.

Then you heard it—a voice, hissing from the shrubs by the south entrance of the school.

"Truck!"

Taylor. Crouched by the hedges. Glaring at you with an expression that managed to be both furious and relieved.

You had never been so glad to be annoyed at someone in your life.

She's heading back inside. Do you follow?
Probably

You grabbed his wrists from behind. Jacket sleeves between your palms and his skin. Good. Smart. You had this.

He twisted with a strength that had no business being in a twelve-year-old and got one hand free. His fingers found the inside of your wrist where your sleeve had ridden up.

The cold hit like a switch being thrown.

You were sitting down. Then you were lying down. The bus ceiling swam above you, fluorescent and flickering. Taylor's voice came from far away, sharp and panicked.

The last thing you felt before the quiet took you was the bus accelerating—Becky somehow back on her feet, foot on the gas, driving with the particular focus of someone who has decided not to think too hard about anything right now.

You'd had a good plan.

Probably just wasn't good enough.

Every Head on the Street

"Taylor!"

The word left your mouth before your brain could vote on it.

The silence that followed lasted exactly one second. Then every sleeper within two blocks turned toward the sound with the slow, synchronized precision of something that had been waiting for a noise to aim at.

Taylor appeared at the school door, stared at you, stared at the street, and mouthed something that was almost certainly not a compliment.

She vanished inside.

You ran. You were fast. You'd always been faster than people expected. But the crowd was everywhere now—closing from both sides, from the parking lot, from the crosswalk. Every gap you aimed for filled before you reached it.

The last gap closed somewhere near the bike rack.

Your bike was right there, too. You'd noticed that. For what it was worth.

The Street

You hit the pavement running and didn't stop. Taylor was beside you—or close enough. The cold bit straight through your shirt. Your lungs begged. None of it mattered.

Two blocks from the school, you cut through a narrow alleyway that opened into a backyard dominated by a massive tree. You skidded to a halt. Taylor nearly ran into you.

Two freshmen boys—twins by the look of them, same face, same matching coats—were already there, already climbing. One looked down.

"Up here," the shorter one said. Not a suggestion.

Taylor looked at the treehouse—weathered wood, uneven ladder, the whole thing creaking in the wind—and then looked at the alley behind you, where shapes were beginning to appear.

She climbed. Fast and focused. Deb—a girl from your class you hadn't even noticed until now—scrambled up behind her, muttering a prayer.

You went last. Arms shaking with every rung. Below, a sleeper groaned, drawn by the noise. An icy hand grazed your shoe. You kicked. Kept climbing.

At the top, the hatch sealed shut behind you. The taller brother weighted it down with an old toolbox.

Five of you. A treehouse barely big enough for three. Somewhere below, the groaning continued—patient, unhurried. Through the gaps in the wood, you could see the street beginning to fill.

"This place feels wrong when it's empty," Deb muttered.

"Understatement," you said.

In the corner, half-buried under boxes, sat a Ouija board and a camcorder with a cracked screen.

You didn't know these guys. But this didn't look like normal kid stuff.

Nothing about today looked like normal kid stuff.

Back Inside

The south entrance was unlocked. Inside, the hallway was dim—red lockers, white cinder block, no windows. With the lights out, it was gloomier than it had any right to be at this hour.

You tripped on something sticky and black on the floor. It clung to your hands when you tried to wipe it off.

"Gross," you said.

Then a third voice came from the dark. Nervous. Brittle.

"Guys?"

A girl in a green puff coat stepped into the dim light. Braces catching the faint glow. You knew her—Deb. Cordial. A few group projects. Different circles, yours being mostly none.

"I didn't see much," she said, words rushing out. "We were at P.E. A parent touched someone's neck. He collapsed. Then he got back up and he was—like them. I ran to the locker room. After the lights went out I couldn't stay."

The three of you stood in the dark while the glass doors rattled at the end of the hall.

"It's like they're sleepwalking," Taylor said. "But contagious."

You raised one finger in the air. "Rule number one—"

"I'll be making the rules," Taylor said flatly. "Rule number one: don't get touched."

She basically plagiarized.

A loud bang echoed from deeper in the school. Then the double doors at the end of the corridor swung open—a teacher, arms outstretched, moving into the hall with the others pushing in behind.

Deb was already moving. "Follow me. I know another way out."
